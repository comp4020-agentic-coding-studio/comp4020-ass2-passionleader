This week opens the course's "common manners" pair: cases that aren't tied
to one physical space, because the pattern they illustrate shows up
anywhere strangers share a room, a footpath, or a queue. Both cases share a
structure — an act that costs someone else something while carrying no
clear moment anyone can point to and call it deliberate.

The first case is the cough. It's unusual among public nuisances in that it
starts out involuntary and everyone in earshot knows it. That is exactly
what makes it interesting: the moment a room decides a particular cough was
"on purpose," the social contract around it changes completely — sympathy
becomes suspicion, and an apology becomes mandatory where it wasn't before.
We catalogue the signals a room actually reads (timing, target, aftermath)
and the deniable coughs a room has already decided were "on purpose"
anyway — not to specify how to make one undetectable, but to analyse why
the line between accident and message is so easy to blur and so hard to
prove.

The second case is litter, specifically threshold litter: what accumulates
in the last few metres before a bin. Littering has almost no immediate
audience — the person who benefits from not carrying a wrapper to a bin is
rarely watched doing it, and the person who eventually pays is rarely the
person who saw it happen. Isolating the act here means reading the
aftermath instead of watching the moment — the same method, applied where
there was never really an audience to begin with.

## Outline

- what makes conduct "incidental": deniability, delayed cost, no audience
- case one: involuntary versus performed sounds, and how a room tells them
  apart
- case two: threshold litter, and what that distance tells you about
  tolerance for inconvenience
- discussion: what should "case study" mean when there's no clear witness
  at all

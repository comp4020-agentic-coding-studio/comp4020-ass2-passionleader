# Common Manners I: Deniable and Incidental Conduct

## SLOP2950 · Week 2

```notes
Two case studies, one underlying question: what happens to a norm when the
act itself can always be waved off as an accident?
```

---

{/* _class: quote */}

> Some acts cost someone else something and still manage to leave no
> fingerprints.

## What makes conduct "incidental"

Not every violation announces itself. Some of this course's most durable
cases work precisely because nobody involved can ever quite prove what
happened.

- **deniability** — the act stays technically indistinguishable from an
  accident
- **delayed cost** — whoever pays for it pays later, and often elsewhere
- **no audience** — the person responsible is rarely watched at the moment
  it matters

> A cough lands exactly as a nearby conversation stops. A wrapper falls a
> few steps short of a bin nobody saw it happen near. Neither act has a
> witness willing to call it anything but an accident.

## Case one: the deniable cough

A cough that a room privately reads as deliberate, while remaining
technically indistinguishable from an accident. Three signals decide which
way a room reads it:

1. **Timing** — does it land inside a pause, or on top of someone speaking
2. **Target** — was it aimed anywhere in particular, or genuinely undirected
3. **Aftermath** — was it acknowledged, or did the room go straight back to
   silence

```notes
These three map directly onto the method: timing and target help you
isolate the act, aftermath is where you describe what happens next.
```

## Bad manner vs. good manner: the cough

**Bad manner:** A student annoyed at the person next to them rustling a bag
of chips lets out one loud, pointed cough exactly as the rustling stops —
then stares straight ahead and says nothing, so nothing about it can be
called anything but a cough.

**Good manner:** That same student leaning over and asking, quietly, "could
you save the rest of those for later?" — the same complaint, stated
instead of performed.

## Case two: threshold litter

What accumulates in the last few metres before a bin — and what that
distance actually tells you about tolerance for inconvenience.

> Empty packaging on the pavement, a public bin plainly visible a few
> steps away — close enough that reaching it would have cost less effort
> than the drop itself.

Not every case is this clean. Whether smaller residue — a piece of chewing
gum, a cigarette butt — counts as "the same kind of littering" as dropped
packaging is a live disagreement; and sometimes the missing bin, not the
person, is the actual constraint — several city transit systems removed
public bins outright for unrelated security reasons, which reshuffles who
the delayed cost lands on.

## Bad manner vs. good manner: the litter

**Bad manner:** Walking past a public bin, close enough to reach it without
breaking stride, and letting an empty drink cup fall to the ground instead —
leaving it for whoever cleans up, or nobody.

**Good manner:** Carrying that same cup the extra few steps to the bin and
setting it inside — a smaller effort than the one just avoided.

## Discussion

Both cases share the same structural gap: no witness at the moment that
matters. What should "case study" mean when the observer and the observed
are so often the same person — and what should it mean when there was
never an observer at all?

## Quiz

**Q1.** What three properties do the deniable cough and threshold litter
share, in this week's framing?

*Deniability (the act stays indistinguishable from an accident), delayed
cost (whoever pays, pays later and elsewhere), and no audience at the
moment it matters.*

---

**Q2.** Which of the three signals for reading a cough (timing, target,
aftermath) is most directly about isolating the act itself, rather than
describing what happened next?

*Timing and target — aftermath belongs to "describe."*

---

**Q3.** Why does the course call littering an "audience-less nuisance"?

*Because the person who benefits from the act is rarely watched doing it —
unlike the cough, which at least has a room full of witnesses who simply
can't prove intent.*

---

**Q4.** In both this week's Bad manner / Good manner pairs, what does the
good-manner version actually cost the person performing it?

*Very little — a quiet sentence instead of a performed cough, a few extra
steps instead of a dropped cup — in both cases, smaller than the effort
just avoided.*

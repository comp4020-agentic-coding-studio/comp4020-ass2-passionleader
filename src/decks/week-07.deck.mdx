## Where the agreement holds, and where it doesn't

- **structurally strong** — a single-file line with one clear front; almost
  never contested
- **structurally ambiguous** — a cluster near a counter, or a fork that
  never cleanly re-merged
- **the counter case** — one line feeding several service points, moving at
  several times the speed of any single lane

> A store counter with no marked lane at all, and a customer who arrived
> last steps straight up to the register the moment it frees — not into an
> empty line, but ahead of several people who were already standing there.

The least contested version of this case: where a single counter serves
multiple staff, the settled answer is one line moving at multiple speeds,
not several separate lines — precisely the setup this week's bad-manner
case exploits by treating an unmarked counter as a free-for-all instead.

---

## The cutting spectrum

From "excuse me, is this the line?" through an exploited ambiguous merge, to
simply walking to the front — each met with a different intensity of
reaction, and none of it specific to any one venue: the same spectrum shows
up at a store counter, a bus stop, a food stall, or a public restroom queue.

```notes
Ask the room which end of the spectrum actually makes them angriest. It's
rarely the most extreme one.
```

## Bad manner vs. good manner

**Bad manner:** At a busy counter with no marked line, a customer who
arrived after everyone else pushes straight up the moment it's free, ahead
of people who were already standing there waiting their turn.

**Good manner:** That same customer checking who else is waiting and
asking, "who's next?" before stepping up — letting the line's own unmarked
order settle the question instead of just claiming it.

## Quiz

**Q1.** Why does the course call a queue "a norm doing the work of a rule"?

*It holds without any physical enforcement — no rope, no ticket, just an
agreement among strangers — the way a rule would if one existed.*

---

**Q2.** What makes a queue "structurally ambiguous," in this week's terms?

*An unclear front or a fork that never cleanly re-merges, as opposed to a
single-file line with an obvious front.*

---

**Q3.** Where does "asking first" sit on the cutting spectrum, and why does
it draw a different reaction than walking straight to the front?

*Near the least confrontational end — it explicitly defers to the group's
judgment instead of assuming the answer, which is exactly what silently
walking to the front skips.*

---

**Q4.** Why does the course treat this week's cutting spectrum as a general
public-space pattern rather than a counter-specific one?

*Because the same escalation — asking first, exploiting an ambiguous
merge, walking straight to the front — shows up at any point where
strangers form an unmarked line, not just at a store register.*
